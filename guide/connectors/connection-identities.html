People and automation connections

“Run this as me” and “let the agent do this” are not credential specifications. A governed call keeps three identities distinct:

  1. the authenticated Daemonloom caller;
  2. the owner and intended use of the selected Connection; and
  3. the credential subject the provider observes—usually a person, app, bot, or service account.

The caller receives authority through a Grant. The provider attributes the action according to the Connection's credential. One identity never silently becomes another.

caller ── Grant ──▶ principal-owned user Connection ──▶ provider sees that user
       └─ Grant ──▶ shared automation Connection ─────▶ provider sees the app or bot

Choose the provider actor deliberately​

IntentConnection shapeTypical provider result
As myselfprincipal-owned delegated OAuth or explicitly chosen personal tokenprovider applies and audits the person's own memberships and permissions
As automationapp installation, service account, group bot, project bot, or another declared automation purposeprovider applies and audits the non-human actor's bounded reach

The wire format does not decide this. Two bearer tokens can represent very different actors, and several GitLab token kinds can share the same prefix. A successful request also cannot safely reclassify a credential after the user selected its purpose.

Slack example​

Slack's four ordinary operations accept either:

  • slack.bot_token, created through the app-install flow and attributed to the workspace app bot; or
  • slack.user_token, created through delegated user consent and attributed to that member.

The required operation scope is the same on either path, but reach can differ because Slack applies bot channel membership to one credential and the person's visibility to the other. Events remain bot-only, Socket Mode remains app-token-only, and Enterprise administration retains its dedicated organization-wide user credential purpose.

Read the Slack connector for the complete source, operation, event, and transport surface.

GitLab example​

GitLab exposes five credential purposes over one selected API surface:

PurposeProvider-visible actorIntended reach
gitlab.oauth_tokenconsenting userthat person's memberships and permissions
gitlab.tokenpersonal-token ownerthat person's memberships and permissions
gitlab.service_account_tokenservice-account botmemberships assigned to the automation identity
gitlab.group_access_tokengroup botone group and its projects, bounded by role
gitlab.project_access_tokenproject botone project, bounded by role

Selected reads accept read_api or api; the selected issue write requires api. A protected Connect Session must prove the effective actor type, actual classic scopes, and expiry. It refuses a human token presented as automation and does not infer group, project, or service-account purpose from a shared token prefix.

Acquisition is not admission​

OAuth declarations tell the connection flow how to request consent and where to observe granted scopes. Protected pasted-token flows likewise collect a credential and may probe the provider from inside Connector custody. Neither path creates operation authority by itself.

The effective runtime surface still requires current generation-bound capability evidence for the exact credential purpose, subject, scopes, and provider context, plus Connection lifecycle, Grant, caller, placement, and policy checks. User and automation Connections never lend scopes to one another and never serve as fallback routes.

Current implementation boundary​

The canonical catalog and public explorer now represent these user and automation alternatives, OAuth acquisition facts, and credential-local scope requirements. The general Connect Session, OAuth callback, actor verification, durable capability-evidence, and invocation runtime remain M3 development work. This page describes the admitted product contract, not a claim that the complete flow is deployed today.

Continue with Connections and grants for lifecycle and revocation behavior.
